About The West Prescott Historic District

The West Prescott Historic District is a National Register district on the hill that rises north from Aspen Creek. The City of Prescott's preservation guide for the district describes it as generally bounded by the rear property lines along both sides of Park Avenue and Country Club Drive, with Gurley Street to the north and Copper Basin Road to the south. It covers 109 parcels across portions of 15 blocks.

The same guide counts 79 contributing buildings and 30 noncontributing ones, plus 16 cast-iron street lamps that are separately listed on the National Register. Construction runs from around the turn of the twentieth century through the 1930s, and the City says the district was almost fully developed by 1939. Styles include Queen Anne, Stick, and Shingle homes, Craftsman bungalows, and Period Revival houses such as Mission, Gothic, and Tudor designs.

What The City Says About Work In This District

This is a National Register district only. The City's guide states that projects here are not subject to review by the Prescott Preservation Commission, though the Commission's input is encouraged and owners are invited to talk with the City's preservation specialist. That is different from the City's local districts, where the Commission reviews exterior work that needs a building permit. The guide also recommends keeping air conditioning units, skylights, and other utilities off roofs that are visible from the street.

Permits are a separate question from preservation review. The City of Prescott's 2026 list of what needs a permit says a water heater replacement needs a permit, while replacing a plumbing fixture does not. It also lists moving or replacing a water or sewer line as permit work. The preservation guide does not address interior plumbing, so confirm anything unusual with the City's Building Division before work starts.

Plumbing In Houses Built Between About 1900 And 1939

A house that has stood for close to a century has usually been repaired, added to, and remodeled more than once. One wall can hold piping from several different periods, and a repair that worked in the kitchen tells you very little about the pipes behind the bathroom. We do not assume what your house contains. We ask when it was built, what has been replaced, and where the main shutoff is, and then we look.

Looking can mean checking exposed piping in a basement or crawlspace, testing water pressure, and running a camera through the sewer line from the cleanout. If the problem is a hidden leak, leak detection pinpoints it without opening walls at random. If the evidence shows a line near the end of its life, we explain the options, from sewer line repair to repiping, and what each would involve before anything is scheduled.

Water Heaters In Older Homes

In an older house the water heater can sit in a basement, a closet, or a converted utility space, and the room, the venting, and the gas or electric supply decide which unit fits. We measure and check those details first. Replacement needs a permit under the City's list, so we plan for that from the start. See our pages on water heater repair and water heater replacement for how the work goes, and tankless water heater service if you are weighing a tankless unit.

Before We Arrive: Water Where It Should Not Be

  • Shut off the main water valve if you can reach it safely. If you do not know where it is, find out now, before there is an emergency.
  • Turn off the power or gas control to the water heater if the leak is near it.
  • Move rugs, furniture, and valuables away from the wet area and photograph the damage for your records.
  • Call us and give the address, roughly when the house was built if you know, and where the water is showing.
