Why Does My Toilet Keep Running? A Troubleshooting Guide

By the Prescott Pro Plumbing team · 2026-09-17.

A toilet that runs on its own, refills without being flushed, or never quite stops making noise is one of the most common plumbing complaints we hear. In most cases the cause is one of a small handful of inexpensive parts rather than anything structural.

The Flapper Is The First Thing To Check.

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush and drops back down to seal the tank once it refills. Over years, this rubber degrades, warps, or accumulates mineral deposits from hard water, all of which prevent it from sealing completely. A flapper that does not seal lets water slowly leak from the tank into the bowl, which the fill valve then senses as a drop in tank level and responds to by refilling, creating the intermittent running sound many homeowners describe. Replacing a flapper is inexpensive and one of the easiest plumbing repairs a homeowner can do themselves, though matching the exact flapper style to your specific toilet model matters more than it might seem.

The Fill Valve Can Also Be The Culprit.

The fill valve controls how much water enters the tank after a flush and shuts off once the tank reaches its set level. A fill valve that has worn out internally sometimes fails to shut off completely, letting a small continuous trickle of water into the tank that then overflows into the overflow tube and down the drain, again sounding like a running toilet. This can be harder to diagnose than a flapper issue since the symptom looks similar, but the fix is different: a fill valve replacement rather than a flapper replacement.

A Simple Test To Tell Them Apart.

Remove the tank lid and watch the water level after the tank finishes refilling from a flush. If the water level continues to rise past the marked fill line and eventually drains into the overflow tube, the fill valve is not shutting off correctly. If the water level looks correct and stable but you can still hear or see water slowly disappearing from the tank over several minutes, the flapper is most likely not sealing. This single observation narrows the repair to the correct part before you spend money on the wrong one.

Mineral Buildup's Role In Hard Water Areas.

Across the Prescott area, hard water accelerates wear on both the flapper and the fill valve compared to a soft-water market, since mineral deposits build up on rubber seals and internal valve components alike. A toilet that has needed more than one flapper replacement in a short period may actually have a fill valve or flush valve seat with mineral buildup preventing a proper seal, which a flapper swap alone will not fix. In that case, cleaning or replacing the flush valve seat itself is the more complete fix.

When It Is More Than A Simple Part Swap.

A toilet that continues running even after both the flapper and fill valve have been replaced may have a cracked flush valve seat or a warped tank-to-bowl gasket, both of which are less common but do happen, particularly on an older toilet. At that point, a full toilet replacement is often more cost-effective than continuing to chase individual component failures on a fixture that has already needed multiple repairs.

Why A Running Toilet Costs More Than It Seems.

A continuously running toilet can waste a genuinely significant amount of water over the course of a month, often enough to produce a noticeable jump on a water bill even though nothing about the toilet looks visibly wrong. This is part of why a running toilet is worth fixing promptly rather than living with it as a minor annoyance, since the ongoing water cost frequently exceeds the price of the part that needs replacing within just a few billing cycles.

A Quiet Toilet Can Still Be Leaking.

Not every running toilet makes an obvious sound. A slow, silent leak past a worn flapper can go unnoticed for months if the flow is gentle enough not to trigger the fill valve audibly. The food coloring test, a few drops in the tank checked after fifteen minutes without flushing, catches this kind of quiet leak by revealing whether color has migrated into the bowl, confirming water is passing through a seal that should be holding it back.

Preventing The Next Running Toilet.

Flapper and fill valve wear is a normal, expected part of toilet ownership rather than a sign something went wrong, and replacing these parts proactively every several years, especially in a hard water area like Prescott, can prevent the surprise of a running toilet altogether. Keeping a spare flapper matched to your toilet's specific model on hand is a small, inexpensive step that turns a future repair into a same-day fix instead of a delayed one.

A Note On Older, Harder-To-Find Toilet Models.

Toilets manufactured before the 1990s sometimes use fittings and flapper sizes that have since become less standard, which can make finding an exact replacement part more difficult than it would be for a newer, more common model. A universal flapper kit can sometimes bridge the gap if a hardware store trip turns up nothing that fits properly. A proper fit matters for a lasting seal, though, and in some cases replacing the whole flush valve assembly is more reliable than forcing an imperfect universal part into an older toilet's specific configuration.
