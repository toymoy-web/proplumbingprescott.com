Tankless Vs Tank Water Heaters At Prescott's Elevation

By the Prescott Pro Plumbing team · 2026-09-17.

Choosing between a tank and tankless water heater comes down to more than just upfront cost. At Prescott's elevation, a few additional factors are worth weighing before making the switch.

How Each Type Actually Works.

A tank water heater stores and continuously reheats a set volume of hot water, ready whenever a fixture calls for it, but limited by that tank's capacity during high-demand periods. A tankless unit heats water on demand as it flows through, using a much higher-output burner or heating element, which means no storage tank and, in theory, unlimited hot water as long as the unit can keep up with the flow rate demanded.

Altitude's Effect On Both Types.

Both gas tank and gas tankless units need altitude-derated burner sizing at Prescott's elevation, so neither type avoids that consideration. Tankless units add a second altitude-sensitive factor: their higher-output burners are more sensitive to combustion air supply, so venting design needs to account for elevation just as much as burner sizing does. Electric versions of either type skip the altitude combustion question entirely, since there is no burner involved.

Real-World Performance Differences.

A tank unit can run out of hot water during sustained high demand, such as back-to-back showers in a larger household, until it has time to reheat. A correctly sized tankless unit does not run out the same way. It does have a maximum flow rate it can heat at once, though, so running multiple hot water fixtures simultaneously can still exceed its capacity in a larger home if it was undersized for the household's actual peak demand.

Maintenance Differences Worth Knowing.

Tankless units need periodic descaling to clear mineral scale from their heat exchanger, which matters more here given the area's hard water than it would in a soft-water market. Tank units need occasional flushing for the same reason. The consequence of skipping it is different, though: a tank with heavy sediment loses efficiency gradually, while a tankless heat exchanger with heavy scale buildup can restrict flow enough to trigger error codes and reduced output more abruptly.

Which One Actually Makes Sense.

A tank unit remains the simpler, lower-upfront-cost option and works well for most households with typical hot water demand patterns. A tankless unit makes more sense for a household with genuinely high or unpredictable hot water demand, or for a smaller mechanical space where a large tank is not practical. We can walk through actual household usage patterns to recommend whichever option fits better, rather than defaulting to whichever unit costs more.

The Upfront Cost Difference, Honestly.

A tankless unit typically costs more upfront than a comparable tank unit, both in equipment and installation, particularly if the switch requires new venting or gas line sizing to support the higher burner output. That higher upfront cost is sometimes offset over time by lower standby energy losses. A tankless unit only heats water when it is actually needed, rather than maintaining a full tank at temperature around the clock, though the actual savings depend heavily on a household's specific usage pattern.

What Happens During A Switch From Tank To Tankless.

Converting from an existing tank system to tankless is not always a simple swap. It often requires larger gas line sizing to support the tankless unit's higher burner output, updated venting to match the new unit's requirements, and sometimes electrical work if the unit needs power for its ignition and controls. We evaluate the existing infrastructure honestly before quoting a conversion, since an incomplete assessment upfront leads to unexpected costs partway through the job.

Expected Lifespan Of Each Type.

A well-maintained tank water heater typically lasts eight to twelve years before replacement becomes the more sensible option. A tankless unit, properly descaled on schedule, often lasts fifteen to twenty years, roughly double a tank's typical service life, which is part of the calculation worth weighing against its higher upfront cost. That longer lifespan assumes the maintenance schedule is actually followed, though, and a neglected tankless unit in a hard water area can fail well before reaching its full potential lifespan.

Space And Installation Considerations.

A tankless unit's smaller physical footprint frees up mechanical space that a bulky tank would otherwise occupy, which matters in a smaller home or a mechanical closet with limited room. This is a real advantage for a renovation or an addition where space is at a premium, separate from any efficiency or performance consideration, and it is worth factoring in for a household planning a larger remodel where every square foot of mechanical space counts.

A Realistic Recommendation For Most Households.

For a typical single-family home with normal hot water habits, a properly sized and correctly altitude-derated tank unit remains a perfectly sound choice, and there is no need to feel pressured into a tankless upgrade just because it is the newer option. Tankless makes the clearest sense for a genuinely high-demand household, a smaller mechanical space, or a homeowner planning to stay in the property long enough to recover the higher upfront cost through the unit's longer working life. We would rather walk through your actual situation honestly than push whichever option carries a higher price tag simply because it costs more to install.

One More Factor: Resale Value.

A tankless water heater is sometimes viewed as a desirable upgrade by buyers in a home sale, though its actual effect on resale value is modest and varies by market. It is a reasonable secondary factor to consider if you are already leaning toward a replacement for other reasons, but it is not, on its own, a strong enough reason to justify the higher cost of switching from a perfectly functional tank system.
